It's funny, I wrote a version of this blog post in May. At that time, it was just a theory that we were never going to be truly reliable again. My take then was based off of the announcement of [Anthropic's Project Glasswing](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing). Basically the idea was we had to hack everything so that we could defend against a future where the speed of attackers was unstoppable. 

I assumed two facts at that time:

1. The rate and amount of code being written has increased 100x. Most of it is generated, most of it is shipped, much of it is barely read.
2. The rate of zero-day discovery is increasing drastically, because finding bugs no longer requires a human.

I underestimated #1 drastically. The rate of code being shipped is regularly taking down Github. We were starting to see that in May, but by mid-summer, we saw just a constant increase. [Github's own blog confirms this](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/) and shows not 100x, but still drastic growth across the platform.

[![github's data for the last three years](https://images.natwelch.com/photos/2026/0SA59PJZW0RPW.webp?w=1200)](https://images.natwelch.com/photos/2026/0SA59PJZW0RPW.webp)

For #2, some people see it as good news: you can now audit a system and find things humans would miss. That is true. But the population of adversarial actors is, and always will be, larger than the population of non-adversarial ones. So the net effect of "anyone can find vulnerabilities cheaply" is not symmetrical. It favors offense.

Which means every piece of software is now, in practice, targeted by something with state-actor-level capability. At [Laurel](https://laurel.ai) our threat model has assumed this for a while. Most apps do not.

And this has become incredibly true. The number of CVEs per day has more than doubled in 2026. [cve.icu](https://cve.icu/) tracks data of all CVEs, and it paints a grim picture.

[![CVE per year](https://images.natwelch.com/photos/2026/0SA5B19B80RPB.png?w=1200)](https://images.natwelch.com/photos/2026/0SA5B19B80RPB.png)

A CVE is not always critical. There are tons that are medium or low. But each CVE disclosed has been found and validated.

## Reliability?

But CVE are talking about security, not reliability right? Well if software is updated constantly, the chance of issues rises.

## What I expect to see

A resurgence of compromised devices in the home. Not just your laptop — your washing machine, your doorbell, your TV, the random IoT junk you forgot you plugged in five years ago. Either being used as a launchpad against other systems, or quietly exfiltrating what's going on inside your house.

A lot of people have already given up on caring about that data. The assumption is that your purchases, your location, your messages are already being slurped up by some combination of banks, ad networks, Facebook, your phone carrier, and pick-your-three-letter-agency. Fine. The change is that now there is enough compute to actually *do something* with all of it. The NSA built giant data centers because they didn't yet know how to process what they had. We are now at the point where analyzing all of it is, arguably, trivial.

Imagine an LLM trained well on a single US citizen — their writing, their voice, their relationships, their financial history. It can impersonate them convincingly enough to fool another citizen into committing a crime, leaking information, or sending money. The systems we use to answer "is this a person or a bot" are not built for that.

I expect at least one Laurel employee to be successfully targeted by a state-level actor in the next six months. Not because we're special — because everyone will be.
